Nets, Subspace Embeddings, and Limited Independence

Why a JL transform for $5^d$ points is enough to embed a whole $d$-dimensional subspace: how big a $\gamma$-net has to be, and how a guarantee on the net extends to every vector. Then CountSketch again, this time asking how much randomness it really needs, which leads to $k$-wise independence and cheap pairwise-independent hash functions.

1. Recap and the question

Definition — $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon,\delta,f)$ A random $S\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}$ such that for any fixed set $V$ of $f$ vectors, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[ \forall\,v,v'\in V:\qquad \langle Sv,Sv'\rangle=\langle v,v'\rangle\pm\varepsilon\,\|v\|_2\,\|v'\|_2 . \]
Definition — $\ell_2$-subspace embedding For $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$, $S$ is a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ $\ell_2$-subspace embedding for the column space $\mathcal C(A)$ if
\[ \forall\,y\in\mathcal C(A):\qquad \|Sy\|_2^2=(1\pm\varepsilon)\,\|y\|_2^2 . \]
Question. A JLT only promises something for a finite set of $f$ points, while $\mathcal C(A)$ contains infinitely many. How do we get from a JLT to a subspace embedding? Answer: apply the JLT to a finite $\gamma$-net of the unit vectors in $\mathcal C(A)$, then show the guarantee spreads to every vector.

2. $\gamma$-nets and their size

Definition — $\gamma$-net A set $\mathcal N\subseteq\mathcal S$ is a $\gamma$-net of $\mathcal S$ if every point of $\mathcal S$ is within $\gamma$ of some net point:
\[ \forall\,x\in\mathcal S\ \ \exists\,x'\in\mathcal N:\qquad \|x-x'\|_2\le\gamma . \]
Question. How large must a $\gamma$-net of the unit sphere $S^{d-1}\subset\mathbb R^d$ be? We want a bound $|\mathcal N|\le f(d)$.

Construction: a maximal packing

Greedily put as many points on $S^{d-1}$ as possible such that every two of them are more than $\gamma$ apart: $\|x-x'\|_2>\gamma$ for all $x\ne x'$. When no more points can be added, every point of the sphere is within $\gamma$ of a chosen point (otherwise we could add it), so the chosen set is a $\gamma$-net.

Volume argument

Put a ball of radius $\gamma/2$ around each net point. Since the points are more than $\gamma$ apart, these balls are disjoint. They all lie inside the ball of radius $1+\gamma/2$ around the origin. Let $V_d$ be the volume of the unit ball in $\mathbb R^d$, so a ball of radius $\rho$ has volume $V_d\,\rho^d$. Comparing volumes,

\[ |\mathcal N|\,V_d\left(\frac\gamma2\right)^{d} \;\le\; \left(1+\frac\gamma2\right)^{d}V_d \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |\mathcal N|\;\le\;\left(1+\frac2\gamma\right)^{d}. \]

With $\gamma=\tfrac12$ this gives $|\mathcal N|\le 5^d$.

3. A net for the column space

For $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$, the set we need to cover is

\[ \mathcal S=\bigl\{\,y\in\mathbb R^n:\ \|y\|_2=1,\ y=Ax \text{ for some } x\in\mathbb R^d\,\bigr\}, \]

the unit vectors of $\mathcal C(A)$. It lives in $\mathbb R^n$, but it is really a sphere of dimension at most $d-1$.

Let $U\in\mathbb R^{n\times d'}$ ($d'=\mathrm{rank}(A)\le d$) have orthonormal columns spanning $\mathcal C(A)$, so $U^\top U=I$. Every $y\in\mathcal S$ is $y=Uz$ for a unique unit vector $z\in S^{d'-1}$, and $U$ preserves distances:

\[ \|Uz_1-Uz_2\|_2=\|z_1-z_2\|_2 . \]

So the path $y\to z\to z'\in$ ($\gamma$-net of $S^{d'-1}$) $\to Uz'$ maps a $\gamma$-net of $S^{d'-1}$ to a $\gamma$-net of $\mathcal S$ of the same size. Hence $\mathcal S$ has a $\tfrac12$-net with at most $5^d$ points.

4. From the net to the whole subspace

Theorem Let $\mathcal N$ be a $\tfrac12$-net of $\mathcal S$. If
\[ \forall\,w,w'\in\mathcal N:\qquad \langle Sw,Sw'\rangle=\langle w,w'\rangle\pm\varepsilon , \]
then $\|Sy\|_2^2=1\pm4\varepsilon$ for every $y\in\mathcal S$, and by scaling, $\|Sy\|_2^2=(1\pm4\varepsilon)\|y\|_2^2$ for every $y\in\mathcal C(A)$.

The hypothesis is exactly what a $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon,\delta,5^d)$ applied to $\mathcal N$ delivers (net points are unit vectors, so $\varepsilon\|w\|\|w'\|=\varepsilon$).

Proof

Write $y$ as a series of net points

Pick $w_0\in\mathcal N$ closest to $y$ and write $y=w_0+\Delta_0$ with $\|\Delta_0\|\le\tfrac12$. The leftover direction $\Delta_0/\|\Delta_0\|$ is again a unit vector in $\mathcal C(A)$, so repeat:

\[ y=w_0+\Delta_0, \qquad \frac{\Delta_0}{\|\Delta_0\|}=w_1+\Delta_1, \qquad \frac{\Delta_1}{\|\Delta_1\|}=w_2+\Delta_2, \qquad\dots \]

with every $\|\Delta_i\|\le\tfrac12$. Unrolling,

\[ y=\sum_{i\ge0}\lambda_i\,w_i, \qquad \lambda_0=1,\quad \lambda_i=\prod_{j<i}\|\Delta_j\|\;\le\;\frac{1}{2^i}. \]

(If some $\Delta_i=0$ the series simply stops.) In particular $\sum_i\lambda_i\le\sum_i 2^{-i}=2$.

Expand $\|Sy\|^2$

\[ \begin{aligned} \|Sy\|_2^2 &=\Bigl\langle S\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_iw_i,\ S\sum_j\lambda_jw_j\Bigr\rangle =\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\lambda_j\,\langle Sw_i,Sw_j\rangle \\ &=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\lambda_j\bigl(\langle w_i,w_j\rangle\pm\varepsilon\bigr) =\underbrace{\Bigl\langle\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_iw_i,\ \sum_j\lambda_jw_j\Bigr\rangle}_{\|y\|_2^2\,=\,1} \;\pm\;\varepsilon\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i\Bigr)^{2} \\ &=1\pm4\varepsilon . \end{aligned} \]

The diagonal terms $i=j$ and the cross terms $i\ne j$ are handled together; both use the net guarantee.

Conclusion. Run the argument with $\varepsilon/4$: a $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon/4,\delta,5^d)$ is a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ $\ell_2$-subspace embedding for $\mathcal C(A)$ with probability $1-\delta$. Since $\log f=d\log5$, this needs only $r=O\!\left(\frac{d+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2}\right)$ rows.
Constants Woodruff states the net size as $(1+4/\gamma)^d$, i.e. $9^d$ for $\gamma=\tfrac12$. The volume argument above gives the slightly sharper $(1+2/\gamma)^d$. Only $\log f=\Theta(d)$ matters for the number of rows, so the base is irrelevant.

5. CountSketch: how much randomness?

Recall CountSketch $S\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}$ for $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$: pick random $h:[n]\to[r]$ and $g:[n]\to\{-1,+1\}$; column $i$ of $S$ has the single non-zero entry $g(i)$ in row $h(i)$. Applying $S$ to a vector $x$ sends $x_i$ to bucket $h(i)$ with sign $g(i)$, so $SA$ costs $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$.

Memory is randomness. $S$ is never stored as a matrix; we store $h$ and $g$. If $h(1),\dots,h(n)$ are fully independent and uniform, writing them down takes $n\log_2 r$ random bits, plus $n$ bits for $g$. That is as much memory as a long vector. Do we need complete randomness?

No. The CountSketch analysis only ever looks at a few coordinates at a time (pairs of collisions for $h$, products of up to four signs for $g$), so limited independence is enough.

6. $k$-wise independence

Definition — $k$-wise independence Random variables $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are $k$-wise independent if every subset of at most $k$ of them is independent: for all $I\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le k$ and all values $x_i$,
\[ \Pr\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{i\in I}\{X_i=x_i\}\Bigr)=\prod_{i\in I}\Pr(X_i=x_i). \]
$2$-wise independence is called pairwise independence.
Theorem — CountSketch with limited independence (Woodruff) If $h$ is $2$-wise independent and $g$ is $4$-wise independent, then CountSketch with $r=O\!\left(\frac{d^2}{\delta\,\varepsilon^2}\right)$ rows is a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ $\ell_2$-subspace embedding for any fixed $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ with probability $1-\delta$.

So the remaining question is how to generate $k$-wise independent values from very few truly random bits.

7. Pairwise-independent bits from a few random bits

Start with $m$ truly random bits $X_1,\dots,X_m$, each $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$. Fully independent bits $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$ would need $n$ random bits (a sample space of size $2^n$). How many pairwise independent bits can $m$ bits produce?

Construction: XOR over subsets

For each non-empty subset $S\subseteq[m]$ define

\[ Y_S=\bigoplus_{i\in S}X_i . \]

There are $2^m-1$ non-empty subsets, so we get $2^m-1$ bits $Y_{S_1},\dots,Y_{S_{2^m-1}}$. Each one is uniform, since flipping any single $X_i$ with $i\in S$ flips $Y_S$.

Why they are pairwise independent

Take $S\ne S'$. Some element $a$ lies in one set but not the other; say $a\in S\setminus S'$. Fix all bits except $X_a$. Then $Y_{S'}$ is already determined, while $Y_S$ is still $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, depending on $X_a$. Hence

\[ \Pr\bigl(Y_S=\alpha\mid Y_{S'}=\beta\bigr)=\tfrac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Pr\bigl(Y_S=0\ \wedge\ Y_{S'}=0\bigr)=\tfrac14=\Pr(Y_S=0)\Pr(Y_{S'}=0). \]
Payoff. $n$ pairwise-independent bits need only $m=\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil$ truly random bits, instead of $n$.
Not 3-wise independent $Y_{\{1\}}=X_1$, $Y_{\{2\}}=X_2$ and $Y_{\{1,2\}}=X_1\oplus X_2$ are pairwise independent, but the third is determined by the first two. Limited independence really is weaker than full independence.

8. Pairwise-independent hash functions

For $h:[n]\to[r]$ we need pairwise-independent values in $[r]$, not just bits. The standard construction (Carter–Wegman) takes a prime $p\ge n$ and two random numbers $x_1,x_2\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}$:

\[ h(i)=\bigl((x_1+i\,x_2)\bmod p\bigr)\bmod r . \]

Why it works

Before the final $\bmod\ r$, consider $h'(i)=(x_1+i\,x_2)\bmod p$. For $i\ne j$, the map $(x_1,x_2)\mapsto\bigl(h'(i),h'(j)\bigr)$ is a bijection of $\mathbb Z_p^2$, because the system $x_1+i\,x_2=u,\ x_1+j\,x_2=v$ has determinant $j-i\ne0\pmod p$ and so exactly one solution. Hence $(h'(i),h'(j))$ is uniform on $\mathbb Z_p^2$: the values $h'(1),\dots,h'(n)$ are pairwise independent and uniform. Reducing $\bmod\ r$ keeps this up to a small bias of order $r/p$, which is negligible for $p\gg r$; in particular $\Pr[h(i)=h(j)]\lesssim 1/r$, which is what CountSketch uses.

Higher independence A random polynomial of degree $k-1$ over $\mathbb Z_p$, $\bigl(\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}x_t\,i^t\bigr)\bmod p$, gives $k$-wise independent values, for the same reason: $k$ distinct evaluation points give an invertible Vandermonde system. A $4$-wise independent $g$ uses a cubic, mapped to $\pm1$.

Summary

Object Truly random bits
Fully random $h:[n]\to[r]$ $n\log_2 r$
$n$ pairwise-independent bits (XOR of subsets) $\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil$
Pairwise-independent $h$ ($x_1+i\,x_2$) $2\log_2 p=O(\log n)$
$k$-wise independent (degree $k-1$ polynomial) $k\log_2 p=O(k\log n)$
Takeaway. A JLT on a $5^d$-point net gives a subspace embedding with $O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ rows. CountSketch trades more rows for one non-zero per column, and with a 2-wise independent $h$ and a 4-wise independent $g$ it can be stored in $O(\log n)$ bits instead of $O(n\log r)$.
