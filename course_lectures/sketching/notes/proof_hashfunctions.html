JLTs as $\ell_2$ subspace embeddings, and limited independence

The net argument that turns a Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform into an $\ell_2$-subspace embedding, following Woodruff, and then two ways to turn a few random bits into exponentially many pairwise independent bits.

These notes fill in the proof sketched in JL Lemma, Subspace Embeddings, and CountSketch. A $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon,\delta,f)$ is a random matrix that preserves all inner products among any fixed set of $f$ vectors, up to $\varepsilon$, with probability at least $1-\delta$. The subspace we care about is infinite, so the argument replaces it by a finite net and then sums a series.

Part I · The subspace embedding

1. How large is a $\gamma$-net of the sphere?

Definition — $\gamma$-net A set $N\subset S^{d-1}$ is a $\gamma$-net of the unit sphere if every point of $S^{d-1}$ is within distance $\gamma$ of some point of $N$.
Lemma — size of a net There is a $\gamma$-net $N\subset S^{d-1}$ with
\[ |N| \le \left(1+\frac{2}{\gamma}\right)^d . \]

Build $N$ greedily: keep adding a point of $S^{d-1}$ as long as it is at distance at least $\gamma$ from every point already chosen. The set you stop with is a $\gamma$-net, because a point farther than $\gamma$ from all of $N$ could still have been added.

The open balls of radius $\gamma/2$ around the net points are disjoint: if two of them met, the net points would be less than $\gamma$ apart. Each ball sits inside the ball of radius $1+\gamma/2$ centered at the origin, since a net point has length $1$. Comparing volumes in $\mathbb{R}^d$,

\[ |N|\cdot V_d\cdot\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^d \le V_d\cdot\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^d , \]

and cancelling $V_d$ gives the claim:

\[ |N| \le \left(\frac{1+\gamma/2}{\gamma/2}\right)^d = \left(1+\frac{2}{\gamma}\right)^d . \]
As written on the page The handwritten note boxes this same bound, but writes the containing radius as $(1+\gamma)/2$. That radius produces $(1+1/\gamma)^d$ instead. The containing ball that matches the boxed bound has radius $1+\gamma/2$. The small balls are disjoint; the volume sum uses that.
The constant we use. Take $\gamma = 1/2$. Then $|N| \le (1+4)^d = 5^d$.

2. A net for one column space

Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ have column space $\mathcal{C}(A)$, and write

\[ \mathcal{S}(A) = \{\, y\in\mathcal{C}(A) : \|y\|_2 = 1 \,\} \]

for the unit sphere in that subspace. Let $U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ have orthonormal columns spanning $\mathcal{C}(A)$, so every $y\in\mathcal{S}(A)$ is $y = Ux$ for a unique $x\in S^{d-1}$, and $U$ preserves distances. If $N$ is a $\gamma$-net of $S^{d-1}$ and $\|x'-x\|_2 < \gamma$, then

\[ \|Ux' - Ux\|_2 = \|x'-x\|_2 < \gamma . \]

So $\{Ux : x\in N\}$ is a $\gamma$-net of $\mathcal{S}(A)$, and it still has size at most $(1+2/\gamma)^d$. For $\gamma = 1/2$ that is at most $5^d$ points, all determined by $A$ before the sketch is drawn.

3. From the net to every vector in the subspace

We want $\|SAx\|_2^2 = (1\pm\varepsilon)\,\|Ax\|_2^2$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$. Both sides scale by $t^2$ when $x$ is replaced by $tx$, so it is enough to prove

\[ \|Sy\|_2^2 = (1\pm\varepsilon)\,\|y\|_2^2 \qquad\text{for every } y\in\mathcal{S}(A). \]

Fix a unit vector $y\in\mathcal{S}(A)$ and a $\tfrac12$-net $N$ of $\mathcal{S}(A)$. There is a net point $w_0$ with $\|\Delta_0\|_2 \le 1/2$, where $\Delta_0 = y - w_0$. The direction of the remainder is again a unit vector in the subspace, so it has its own net point, and so on:

\[ y = w_0 + \|\Delta_0\|\, w_1 + \|\Delta_0\|\,\|\Delta_1\|\, w_2 + \cdots = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \lambda_i w_i . \]

Here each $w_i\in N$ is a unit vector, $\lambda_0 = 1$, and $\lambda_i \le 2^{-i}$. The remainder after $t$ steps has length at most $2^{-t}$ and disappears in the limit, so the series really is $y$.

Apply a matrix $S$ that is a $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon', \delta, 5^d)$ for this fixed net. On the success event, every pair of net points satisfies $\langle Sw_i, Sw_j\rangle = \langle w_i, w_j\rangle \pm \varepsilon'$, because the net vectors are unit. Expand the sketched norm:

\[ \begin{aligned} \|Sy\|_2^2 &= \Bigl\langle S\sum_i \lambda_i w_i,\; S\sum_j \lambda_j w_j \Bigr\rangle \\ &= \sum_{i,j} \lambda_i\lambda_j\, \langle Sw_i, Sw_j\rangle \\ &= \sum_{i,j} \lambda_i\lambda_j\, \langle w_i, w_j\rangle \;\pm\; \varepsilon' \sum_{i,j} \lambda_i\lambda_j \\ &= \Bigl\langle \sum_i \lambda_i w_i,\; \sum_j \lambda_j w_j \Bigr\rangle \;\pm\; \varepsilon' \Bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\Bigr)^2 \\ &= \|y\|_2^2 \;\pm\; \varepsilon' \Bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\Bigr)^2 . \end{aligned} \]

The scalar series is a geometric series, $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \lambda_i \le \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} 2^{-i} = 2$, so its square is at most $4$. Therefore

\[ \|Sy\|_2^2 = 1 \pm 4\varepsilon' . \]

Feed the JLT the smaller accuracy $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon/4$. Then $\|Sy\|_2^2 = 1\pm\varepsilon$ for every unit $y$ in the column space, on a single event of probability at least $1-\delta$. Scaling gives the subspace embedding for every $x$.

Theorem A $\mathrm{JLT}(\varepsilon/4,\ \delta,\ 5^d)$ is a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$ $\ell_2$-subspace embedding for the column space of any fixed $n\times d$ matrix. The $5^d$ is the size of the $\tfrac12$-net.
Why the net has to be fixed first The JLT promise is for one set of $5^d$ vectors chosen without looking at $S$. The net depends only on $A$. It does not depend on the sketch, so the failure probability stays $\delta$ rather than having to cover infinitely many $y$ by a union bound.
Part II · Limited independence

4. Random bits for a CountSketch

A CountSketch matrix has a single nonzero entry in each column, equal to $\pm 1$. With

\[ r = O\Bigl(\frac{d^2}{\varepsilon^2\delta}\Bigr) \]

rows it is itself an $\ell_2$-subspace embedding; see the CountSketch section of the companion notes. The handwritten page writes this matrix as $k\times d$ with the same row bound. In the notation of those notes the matrix is $r\times n$: $n$ columns, one for each coordinate of the ambient space, and $d$ is the dimension of the subspace being embedded.

If the row choices are mutually independent, each of the $n$ columns picks one of $r$ rows and needs $\log_2 r$ bits. The page boxes this cost for an $m\times n$ sketch:

\[ n\log_2 m \qquad\text{random bits}. \]

Independent signs cost $n$ further bits. Either way the cost grows with $n$. The rest of the notes replaces that with a seed whose length does not grow with $n$.

5. $k$-wise independence

Definition Random variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are $k$-wise independent if for every distinct indices $i_1,\ldots,i_k$ and every values $y_1,\ldots,y_k$,
\[ \Pr(X_{i_1}=y_1,\ldots,X_{i_k}=y_k) = \Pr(X_{i_1}=y_1)\cdots\Pr(X_{i_k}=y_k). \]
Pairwise independence is the case $k=2$. Full independence is $k=n$.

A CountSketch does not need full independence. It is enough that the row hash be $2$-wise independent and the signs be $4$-wise independent. Both can be built from the constructions below.

6. Exponentially many pairwise bits

Start with $m$ independent fair bits $X_1,\ldots,X_m\in\{0,1\}$. For every nonempty subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$ define

\[ Y_S = \bigoplus_{j\in S} X_j . \]

There are $2^m - 1$ nonempty subsets, so $m$ random bits produce $2^m - 1$ bits. The claim is that the family $\{Y_S\}$ is pairwise independent, and each $Y_S$ is itself a fair bit.

Two bits give three

$X_1$ $X_2$ $Y_{\{1\}}=X_1$ $Y_{\{2\}}=X_2$ $Y_{\{1,2\}}=X_1\oplus X_2$
00000
01011
10101
11110

Each pair of columns is uniform on $\{0,1\}^2$, so the three bits are pairwise independent. They are not mutually independent: $Y_{\{1,2\}}$ is determined by the other two. That is the gap between $2$-wise and $3$-wise independence, and it is why the stretch can be exponential. A fully independent family of length $2^m-1$ would have needed $2^m-1$ fresh bits.

Why any two are independent

Take two distinct nonempty subsets $S$ and $T$. Their symmetric difference is nonempty, so some index lies in one and not the other. Say $k\in S\setminus T$. Fix every bit except $X_k$. Then $Y_T$ does not depend on $X_k$, while flipping $X_k$ flips $Y_S$. Exactly one of the two choices of $X_k$ makes $Y_S$ equal any prescribed bit, so

\[ \Pr(Y_S = y \mid Y_T = y') = \tfrac12 . \]

The same argument with $T$ a singleton shows that $Y_T$ is a fair bit. Therefore

\[ \Pr(Y_S = y,\ Y_T = y') = \Pr(Y_S = y\mid Y_T = y')\,\Pr(Y_T = y') = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14 . \]

7. Pairwise hashes over a prime field

The XOR construction stretches bits. A CountSketch also needs random values in a larger alphabet, one row index per column. Let $p$ be prime and draw two uniform field elements

\[ X_1, X_2 \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\} . \]

For each nonzero index $i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}$ set

\[ Y_i = (X_1 + i\, X_2) \bmod p . \]

These are $p-1$ random numbers in $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$, built from $2\log_2 p$ bits. Including $i = 0$, which just returns $X_1$, makes $p$ of them. The page asks why they are uniform and pairwise independent.

Why one value is uniform

Fix $i$ and a target $y$. For each of the $p$ choices of $X_2$ there is exactly one $X_1$ with $X_1 + i X_2 \equiv y \pmod p$, namely $X_1 \equiv y - i X_2$. So $p$ of the $p^2$ seeds work:

\[ \Pr(Y_i = y) = \frac{p}{p^2} = \frac{1}{p}. \]

Why two values are independent

Fix $i\ne j$ and targets $y_i, y_j$. The page writes the two equations

\[ X_1 + i X_2 \equiv y_i, \qquad X_1 + j X_2 \equiv y_j \pmod p . \]

Subtracting gives $(i-j)X_2 \equiv y_i - y_j$. Since $p$ is prime and $i\ne j$, the difference $i-j$ has an inverse, and there is exactly one solution $(X_1, X_2)$. One seed out of $p^2$ produces the pair, so

\[ \Pr(Y_i = y_i,\ Y_j = y_j) = \frac{1}{p^2} = \Pr(Y_i = y_i)\,\Pr(Y_j = y_j). \]
The same idea, $k$ at a time Replacing the linear polynomial by $Y_i = X_1 + i X_2 + \cdots + i^{k-1} X_k \bmod p$ makes any $k$ of the $Y_i$ uniform on $\mathbb{F}_p^k$. The seed is $k$ field elements, which is $O(k\log p)$ bits, and the number of outputs can be as large as $p$. That is a $k$-wise independent family. CountSketch uses the $k=2$ case for the row hash and the $k=4$ case for the signs.
