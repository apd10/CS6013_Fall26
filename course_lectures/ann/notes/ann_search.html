Approximate Nearest Neighbour Search: PQ, ScaNN, and SOAR

How to search billions of vectors without scanning them in full precision. Product quantization compresses vectors into short codes and estimates distances from lookup tables. ScaNN changes the quantization loss so that it protects the inner products that matter for maximum inner product search. SOAR gives each point a second, deliberately different partition so that a failure in the first one is less likely to repeat in the second.

Part I · Product Quantization (Jégou, Douze, Schmid)

Given a database $\mathcal Y\subset\mathbb R^D$ of $n$ vectors and a query $x\in\mathbb R^D$, the nearest-neighbour problem is

\[ \mathrm{NN}(x)=\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal Y} d(x,y), \qquad d(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2 . \]

Exhaustive search costs $O(nD)$. Tree methods such as KD-trees are no faster than brute force in high dimension. LSH and FLANN-style methods speed up the search, but they keep the original vectors in memory for a final re-ranking step, which limits how many vectors fit on one machine. The goal here is to make the search fast and the memory small: each vector is stored as a short code, and distances are estimated from the codes.

1. Vector quantization

Definition — quantizer A quantizer is a map $q:\mathbb R^D\to\mathcal C=\{c_i : i\in\mathcal I\}$, with $\mathcal I=\{0,\dots,k-1\}$. The $c_i$ are centroids and $\mathcal C$ is the codebook of size $k$. The Voronoi cell of index $i$ is
\[ \mathcal V_i=\{x\in\mathbb R^D : q(x)=c_i\}. \]
The $k$ cells partition $\mathbb R^D$.

Quantizer quality is measured by the mean squared error

\[ \mathrm{MSE}(q)=\mathbb E_X\!\left[d(q(x),x)^2\right] =\int p(x)\,d(q(x),x)^2\,dx . \]

Lloyd optimality conditions

  1. Nearest centroid. Each vector goes to its closest centroid: $q(x)=\arg\min_{c_i\in\mathcal C} d(x,c_i)$. As a result, cells are bounded by hyperplanes.
  2. Centroid is the cell mean. $c_i=\mathbb E_X[x\mid i]=\int_{\mathcal V_i}p(x)\,x\,dx$ (normalised by the cell's probability).

Alternating between these two steps is Lloyd's algorithm, i.e. $k$-means. It finds a local optimum of the MSE. Two quantities are used below: the probability $p_i=\Pr(q(x)=c_i)$ of a cell, and the mean distortion inside it,

\[ \xi(q,c_i)=\frac{1}{p_i}\int_{\mathcal V_i} d(x,q(x))^2\,p(x)\,dx, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(q)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}p_i\,\xi(q,c_i). \]

Storing an index costs $\lceil\log_2 k\rceil$ bits.

2. Product quantizers

The problem with plain $k$-means. A 64-bit code for a 128-dimensional SIFT vector (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform, a local image descriptor; only 0.5 bit per component) needs $k=2^{64}$ centroids. We cannot train that many with Lloyd's algorithm, and we cannot even store the $D\times k$ floats.

Split $x$ into $m$ subvectors $u_1(x),\dots,u_m(x)$, each of dimension $D^*=D/m$, and quantize each one with its own small quantizer $q_j$:

\[ \underbrace{x_1,\dots,x_{D^*}}_{u_1(x)},\;\dots,\; \underbrace{x_{D-D^*+1},\dots,x_D}_{u_m(x)} \;\longmapsto\; q_1\bigl(u_1(x)\bigr),\dots,q_m\bigl(u_m(x)\bigr). \]

The codebook is the Cartesian product $\mathcal C=\mathcal C_1\times\cdots\times\mathcal C_m$, and a centroid is a concatenation of sub-centroids. If every sub-quantizer has $k^*$ centroids, the total number of centroids is

\[ k=(k^*)^m, \qquad\text{code length } \ell=m\log_2 k^* \text{ bits}. \]

We only store the $m\times k^*$ sub-centroids, i.e. $m\,D^*\,k^*=k^*D$ floats. Training is $m$ independent $k$-means runs with $k^*$ centroids of dimension $D^*$. Since the subspaces are orthogonal, the distortion adds up: $\mathrm{MSE}(q)=\sum_j\mathrm{MSE}(q_j)$.

Quantizer Codebook memory Assignment cost
$k$-means $kD$ $kD$
Hierarchical $k$-means (branching $b_f$, height $l$) $\frac{b_f}{b_f-1}(k-1)\,D$ $l\,D$
Product $k$-means $m k^* D^*=k^{1/m}D$ $m k^* D^*=k^{1/m}D$
Choosing $m$ and $k^*$ For a fixed bit budget, a few sub-quantizers with many centroids beat many sub-quantizers with few centroids. At the extreme $m=1$, PQ is plain $k$-means; at $m=D$ it is a scalar quantizer. Large $k^*$ raises the assignment cost and the lookup-table size, so $k^*=256$, $m=8$ (64-bit codes, one byte per sub-code) is a common choice.
Balancing subspaces Each subvector should carry comparable energy. A random rotation before quantizing would ensure this, but for SIFT and GIST (a 960-dimensional global descriptor of a whole image's scene layout) it hurts, because neighbouring components are correlated and are better quantized together.
Distances from codes For a query $x$, precompute the table $d\bigl(u_j(x),c_{j,i}\bigr)^2$ for every sub-quantizer $j$ and sub-centroid $i$ ($m\times k^*$ entries). The squared distance to any encoded $y$ is then $\sum_j d\bigl(u_j(x),q_j(u_j(y))\bigr)^2$: $m$ table lookups and additions, with no decoding.

3. How is PQ relevant to attention?

During decoding, the new token's attention query $\mathbf q\in\mathbb R^{d_h}$ (bold, to distinguish it from a quantizer $q$) attends to the cached keys $k_1,\dots,k_s$ of all $s$ previous tokens:

\[ a_i=\mathrm{softmax}_i\!\left(\frac{\mathbf q^\top k_i}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right). \]

Attention scores are heavy-tailed, so selective attention keeps only the top-$k$ tokens by $\mathbf q^\top k_i$. Finding them is a maximum inner product search over the keys. PQCache does this search with PQ.

Prefill: build the codebook on the keys

For each layer and KV head, split each key into $m$ sub-vectors of dimension $d_m=d_h/m$ and run $k$-means separately in each subspace with $2^b$ centroids:

\[ \text{centroids } C\in\mathbb R^{m\times 2^b\times d_m}, \qquad \text{codes } \mathrm{code}\in\{1,\dots,2^b\}^{s\times m}\ (b \text{ bits each}). \]

Decode: approximate scores from a lookup table

Split $\mathbf q$ the same way into $\mathbf q^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf q^{(m)}$ and multiply it with the centroids once, an $(m,1,d_m)\times(m,d_m,2^b)\to(m,2^b)$ product:

\[ T[j,c]=\bigl\langle \mathbf q^{(j)},\,C_{j,c}\bigr\rangle, \qquad \mathbf q^\top k_i\;\approx\;\sum_{j=1}^{m}T\bigl[j,\ \mathrm{code}_{i,j}\bigr]. \]

Gather and sum over the codes, take the top-$k$ approximate scores, fetch only those tokens' exact keys and values from CPU memory, and run attention on them together with the initial and most recent (local) tokens.

Cost Per head, scoring costs $O(2^b d_h + ms)$ instead of $O(s\,d_h)$ for exact $\mathbf q^\top K$, and the codes are only $\frac{mb}{16\,d_h}$ of the FP16 keys' size. With the default $m=2$, $b=6$, $d_h=128$ this is under $1/128$.

4. MIPS and why reconstruction loss is the wrong target

In recommendation, retrieval, and extreme classification, queries and items are embedded in the same space and we want the item with the largest inner product. This is maximum inner product search (MIPS):

\[ x^*=\arg\max_{x_i\in X}\ \langle q,x_i\rangle . \]

A fast MIPS system has two parts: partitioning (score fewer items, e.g. trees, LSH, graphs, or an inverted file) and quantization (score each item faster). ScaNN improves the quantization part. With quantized points, all $n$ inner products cost $O(kd+mn)$ via lookup tables (with $m$ codebooks of $k$ codewords each), instead of $O(nd)$.

Reconstruction loss is what isotropic queries imply

If we minimize the expected inner-product error over queries and assume $\mathbb E[qq^\top]=\sigma^2 I$, then

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E_q\langle q,x_i-\tilde x_i\rangle^2 =\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E_q(x_i-\tilde x_i)^\top qq^\top(x_i-\tilde x_i) =\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n\|x_i-\tilde x_i\|^2 . \]

So the usual $k$-means / PQ reconstruction loss is the natural objective under this assumption. But it averages over all $(q,x)$ pairs equally.

Key observation. Not all pairs matter equally. For MIPS, errors on pairs with a high inner product decide the top results; errors on low inner-product pairs hardly matter. So $x$ should be quantized to be accurate for the queries that score it highly.

5. Score-aware quantization loss

Definition — score-aware loss For a datapoint $x_i$, its quantization $\tilde x_i$, and a weight function $w:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^{+}$,
\[ \ell(x_i,\tilde x_i,w)=\mathbb E_{q\sim\mathcal Q}\!\left[ w\bigl(\langle q,x_i\rangle\bigr)\,\langle q,x_i-\tilde x_i\rangle^2\right]. \]
Without loss of generality $\|q\|=1$, and with no prior on queries we take $q$ uniform on the unit sphere.

Parallel and orthogonal residuals

Split the residual $x_i-\tilde x_i$ along and across $x_i$:

\[ r_\parallel(x_i,\tilde x_i)=\frac{\langle x_i-\tilde x_i,\,x_i\rangle}{\|x_i\|^2}\,x_i, \qquad r_\perp(x_i,\tilde x_i)=(x_i-\tilde x_i)-r_\parallel(x_i,\tilde x_i). \]
Theorem — the loss decomposes If $q$ is uniform on the unit sphere in $\mathbb R^d$, then for any $w$,
\[ \ell(x_i,\tilde x_i,w)=h_\parallel\,\|r_\parallel\|^2+h_\perp\,\|r_\perp\|^2, \]
\[ h_\parallel=\int_0^\pi w(\|x_i\|\cos\theta)\bigl(\sin^{d-2}\theta-\sin^d\theta\bigr)\,d\theta, \qquad h_\perp=\frac1{d-1}\int_0^\pi w(\|x_i\|\cos\theta)\,\sin^d\theta\,d\theta . \]
On the scaling The main text of the paper writes $h_\parallel$ with a factor $(d-1)$ and $h_\perp$ without $\frac1{d-1}$; the appendix uses the form above. Both differ by the same overall factor $(d-1)$, so the ratio $h_\parallel/h_\perp$, which is all that matters, is the same.

If $w(t)=0$ for $t<0$ and $w$ is non-decreasing for $t\ge0$ (it cares more about high scores), then $h_\parallel\ge h_\perp$, with equality only for constant $w$. Error along $x$ then costs more than error across it, so the loss is anisotropic. Writing $\eta:=h_\parallel/h_\perp\ge1$, the loss is proportional to $\eta\|r_\parallel\|^2+\|r_\perp\|^2$.

6. Anisotropic vector quantization

Definition — anisotropic VQ Given $x_1,\dots,x_n$, weights $h_{i,\parallel},h_{i,\perp}$, and $k$ codewords, find
\[ \min_{c_1,\dots,c_k}\sum_i\ \min_{\tilde x_i\in\{c_1,\dots,c_k\}} h_{i,\parallel}\|r_\parallel(x_i,\tilde x_i)\|^2 +h_{i,\perp}\|r_\perp(x_i,\tilde x_i)\|^2 . \]
With $h_\parallel=h_\perp$ this is exactly $k$-means.

A Lloyd-style algorithm

  1. Initialize codewords as random datapoints.
  2. Assign each $x_i$ to the codeword with the smallest anisotropic loss (enumerate all $k$).
  3. Update each codeword to minimize the loss over its partition $X_j$.
  4. Repeat until a fixed point. The loss never increases and there are finitely many partitions, so it converges.
Theorem — closed-form codeword update
\[ c_j^*=\left(I\sum_{x_i\in X_j}h_{i,\perp} +\sum_{x_i\in X_j}\frac{h_{i,\parallel}-h_{i,\perp}}{\|x_i\|^2}\,x_ix_i^\top\right)^{-1} \sum_{x_i\in X_j}h_{i,\parallel}\,x_i . \]

Why. For one point, expanding $\|r_\parallel\|^2$ and $\|r_\perp\|^2$ in terms of $\tilde x$ gives

\[ h_\parallel\|r_\parallel\|^2+h_\perp\|r_\perp\|^2 =\tilde x^\top\!\left((h_\parallel-h_\perp)\frac{xx^\top}{\|x\|^2}+h_\perp I\right)\tilde x -2h_\parallel\,x^\top\tilde x+h_\parallel\|x\|^2 . \]

Summed over the partition, this is a quadratic in $\tilde x$. Since $h_\parallel\ge h_\perp$, the matrix is positive definite, so the loss is convex and setting the gradient to zero gives $c_j^*$. With $h_\parallel=h_\perp$ it reduces to a weighted mean, and with $w\equiv1$ to the ordinary $k$-means mean.

Part III · SOAR

SOAR stands for Spilling with Orthogonality-Amplified Residuals. Its contributions:

  1. demonstrate a weakness of VQ-based ANN indices;
  2. identify that failure probabilities under spilling are correlated;
  3. introduce SOAR to break that correlation.

7. Why VQ-based search fails

Assume $c$ centroids $\{c_1,\dots,c_c\}$, stacked as $C\in\mathbb R^{c\times d}$, and the assignment

\[ \pi(u):\mathbb R^d\to\{1,\dots,c\}, \qquad \pi(u)=\arg\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,c\}}\|u-c_i\| . \]

Each centroid owns a partition: the list of points assigned to it. At query time we score partitions by $\langle q,c_i\rangle$ and search only the top $k'$ of them. Let $r=x-c_{\pi(x)}$ be the residual. Then

\[ \langle q,x\rangle-\langle q,c_{\pi(x)}\rangle=\langle q,x-c_{\pi(x)}\rangle=\langle q,r\rangle, \qquad\text{so}\qquad q^\top c_{\pi(x)}=q^\top x-q^\top r . \]
The failure mode. If $x$ is a true nearest neighbour of $q$, we want its partition to rank high, not low. If $q$ and $r$ are aligned, $q^\top r$ is large, so $q^\top c_{\pi(x)}$ is low, the partition misses the top $k'$, and $x$ is never scored. A bad partition rank means a harder search.

Since

\[ q^\top r=\|q\|\,\|r\|\cos\theta, \]

both $\|r\|$ and $\theta$ (the angle between $q$ and $r$) contribute to high alignment. To reduce $q^\top r$ we can make $\|r\|$ small or $\cos\theta$ small. Standard VQ training (the $k$-means loss) already minimizes $\|r\|$, so SOAR targets the $\cos\theta$ term.

8. Spilled assignment and correlated failures

A high $\cos\theta$ can be mitigated by assigning each datapoint $x$ to a second VQ partition $\pi'(x)$, with residual $r'=x-c_{\pi'(x)}$ and angle $\theta'$ between $q$ and $r'$. This is a spilled assignment: $x$ is stored in two partitions.

  • It gives the search a second chance, if $\theta$ and $\theta'$ are independently distributed.
  • Spilling fails when $\theta$ and $\theta'$ are correlated: when the primary partition fails, the secondary fails too.

To measure this, take queries $q_1,\dots,q_k$, compute $\cos\theta$ and $\cos\theta'$ for each, and find the correlation between them.

Takeaway Naively spilling to the next-closest centroid buys less than it seems: the second residual tends to point in a similar direction to the first, so the same queries hurt both assignments.

9. SOAR: orthogonality-amplified residuals

Assume the VQ centroids $C$ and the primary assignments $\pi$ are fixed. The goal of the second assignment $\pi'$ is a low quantized score error $q^\top r'$, but not uniformly over queries:

  • if the primary assignment already does well for $q$ (small $\cos\theta$), the secondary need not do much;
  • if the primary assignment is bad for $q$ ($q$ aligned with $r$), the secondary must do well.

So the loss weights each query's error on $r'$ by how badly it is aligned with $r$, using a weighting function $w(\cdot):\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^{\ge0}$:

\[ \mathcal L(r';r,\mathcal Q) =\mathbb E_{q\in\mathcal Q}\!\left[w\!\left(\frac{q^\top r}{\|q\|\,\|r\|}\right)(q^\top r')^2\right] =\mathbb E_{q\in\mathcal Q}\!\left[w(\cos\theta)\,(q^\top r')^2\right]. \]

This mirrors ScaNN's score-aware loss (Section 5), with the primary residual $r$ playing the role that $x$ played there.

Theorem — SOAR loss For the weight function $w(t)=|t|^\lambda$ and queries uniform on $S^{d-1}$,
\[ \mathcal L(r';r,\mathcal Q)\;\propto\;\|r'\|^2+\lambda\,\bigl\|\mathrm{proj}_r\,r'\bigr\|^2, \qquad \mathrm{proj}_r\,r'=\frac{\langle r',r\rangle}{\|r\|^2}\,r . \]

Why (sketch)

Decompose $r'$ and $q$ relative to the direction of $r$: $r'=r'_\parallel+r'_\perp$ and $q=q_\parallel+q_\perp$. For a unit query, $\|q_\parallel\|=|\cos\theta|$ and $\|q_\perp\|=|\sin\theta|$. Then

\[ q^\top r'=\langle q_\parallel+q_\perp,\;r'_\parallel+r'_\perp\rangle =\langle q_\parallel,r'_\parallel\rangle+\langle q_\perp,r'_\perp\rangle . \]

Squaring and averaging, the cross term vanishes by symmetry:

\[ \mathcal L =\underbrace{\mathbb E\bigl[|\cos\theta|^{\lambda}\cos^2\theta\bigr]}_{a}\,\|r'_\parallel\|^2 +\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{d-1}\mathbb E\bigl[|\cos\theta|^{\lambda}\sin^2\theta\bigr]}_{b}\,\|r'_\perp\|^2 =b\,\|r'\|^2+(a-b)\,\|r'_\parallel\|^2 . \]

Since $r'_\parallel=\mathrm{proj}_r\,r'$, the loss is proportional to $\|r'\|^2+\tfrac{a-b}{b}\|\mathrm{proj}_r r'\|^2$. For uniform $q$, $\cos^2\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac{d-1}2\bigr)$, and with $M=\mathbb E|\cos\theta|^\lambda$ the Beta moments give $a=M\frac{1+\lambda}{d+\lambda}$ and $b=M\frac{1}{d+\lambda}$, so $\frac{a-b}{b}=\lambda$ exactly. Larger $\lambda$ means the weight concentrates more on queries aligned with $r$.

The modified assignment

The modification is only to the assignment loss; centroids are unchanged. The spilled assignment $\pi'(u):\mathbb R^d\to\{1,\dots,c\}$ is chosen to minimize

\[ \pi'(x)=\arg\min_{i\ne\pi(x)}\ \|x-c_i\|^2+\lambda\,\bigl\|\mathrm{proj}_r(x-c_i)\bigr\|^2, \qquad r=x-c_{\pi(x)} . \]
Intuition. The $\|r'\|^2$ term keeps the secondary centroid close, as usual. The $\lambda\|\mathrm{proj}_r r'\|^2$ term penalizes any part of $r'$ that points along $r$, which pushes $r'$ to be orthogonal to $r$. A query aligned with $r$ (the case where the primary fails) is then nearly orthogonal to $r'$, so $q^\top r'$ is small and the secondary partition ranks high. This is what breaks the correlation between $\theta$ and $\theta'$.
